Praying while travelling

Shortening and combining prayers

Two concessions make prayer easier on a journey. Qaṣr, shortening, means praying the four-rakʿah prayers as two. Jamʿ, combining, means praying two prayers together in the time of one. The four Sunni schools all accept shortening. They differ on combining, and on the details of both.

    Shortening (qaṣr)

    “When you travel throughout the land, there is no blame upon you for shortening the prayer” (Qurʾān 4:101). ʿĀʾishah said the prayer was first prescribed as two rakʿahs; it was kept that way for travel and made complete for those at home (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1090).

    • Which prayers: Ẓuhr, ʿAṣr and ʿIshāʾ become two rakʿahs. Fajr stays two and Maghrib stays three. Ibn Qudāmah reports agreement that Fajr and Maghrib are not shortened (al-Mughnī 2:197).
    • When it starts: once you have left the built-up area of your town (al-Hidāyah 1:80; al-Risālah p. 45; Minhāj p. 44). Anas prayed four rakʿahs of Ẓuhr with the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, and two rakʿahs of ʿAṣr at Dhul-Ḥulayfah, outside it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1089). Whether an airport counts as inside or outside your town is a modern question; ask your local scholar.
    • Is it required? The Ḥanafī school holds that the traveller’s obligation is two rakʿahs (al-Hidāyah 1:80). Khalīl states that shortening is a sunnah for the traveller (Mukhtaṣar Khalīl p. 43), and the well-known Mālikī position is described as a strong sunnah (al-Zuḥaylī 2:1340). In the Ḥanbalī school the traveller may shorten or pray in full, and shortening is better (al-Mughnī 2:197, 2:199). Al-Nawawī says shortening is better than praying in full when the journey reaches three marḥalahs (Minhāj p. 45), so in the Shāfiʿī school the preference depends on the length of the journey.
    • Behind a resident imam: a traveller who prays behind an imam praying in full prays in full too, in the Ḥanafī, Shāfiʿī and Ḥanbalī texts (al-Hidāyah 1:81; Minhāj p. 45; al-Mughnī 2:209). Ibn Qudāmah reports that Mālik held this only if the traveller catches at least one rakʿah with the imam; if he catches less, he shortens (al-Mughnī 2:209).

    How far is a journey?

    The schools set a minimum distance, measured in old units.

    SchoolMinimum distanceStaying in one placeShortening
    ḤanafīThree days’ travel, at the pace of camels and walking (al-Hidāyah 1:80)Intending to stay 15 days or more ends it (al-Hidāyah 1:80)The traveller’s obligation is two (al-Hidāyah 1:80)
    MālikīFour burud, which al-Risālah gives as 48 miles (al-Risālah p. 45; Khalīl p. 43)Intending to stay four full days, or twenty prayers, ends it (al-Risālah p. 46; Khalīl p. 43)Sunnah; described as a strong sunnah (Khalīl p. 43; al-Zuḥaylī 2:1340)
    Shāfiʿī48 Hāshimī miles, two marḥalahs (Minhāj p. 44)Intending to stay four days, not counting the days you arrive and leave, ends it (Minhāj p. 44)Permitted; better when the journey reaches three marḥalahs (Minhāj p. 45)
    ḤanbalīSixteen farsakh, or 48 Hāshimī miles (al-Mughnī 2:188)Intending to stay for more than twenty-one prayers ends it (al-Mughnī 2:212)Permitted; better (al-Mughnī 2:197, 2:199)

    Converting these old measures into kilometres and miles is done differently by different writers. One modern reference, Wahbah al-Zuḥaylī, puts the four burud of the Mālikī, Shāfiʿī and Ḥanbalī schools at about 89 km (roughly 55 miles), and expresses the Ḥanafī three days in hours of travel rather than a single distance (al-Fiqh al-Islāmī wa-Adillatuh 2:1343). Ask your local scholar which figure they follow.

    Some scholars held that there is no fixed distance at all. Ibn Qudāmah, after setting out the Ḥanbalī position, wrote that he saw no proof for the set distances and that “the evidence is with those who allow shortening for every traveller” unless there is consensus otherwise (al-Mughnī 2:190). Ibn Taymiyyah held that the Prophet ﷺ set no limit of time or distance for travel, and that what counts as a journey is what people customarily call one (Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā 24:12, 24:40). These are reported views; the four schools’ own positions are in the table.

    The reports behind the stay rules differ too. Anas said they prayed two rakʿahs on the trip from Madinah to Makkah and back, including ten days in Makkah (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1081). Ibn ʿAbbās reported a stay of nineteen days with shortened prayers (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1080).

    If you are stuck waiting day by day, not knowing when you’ll leave, the schools allow you to keep shortening, with limits that differ. In al-Hidāyah, someone who stays expecting to leave “today or tomorrow” keeps shortening even for years (al-Hidāyah 1:80). Khalīl says staying does not end travel without the intention to stay, even if departure is delayed (Khalīl p. 43). Al-Nawawī gives eighteen days as the school’s preferred limit (Minhāj p. 44). Ibn Qudāmah explains the Ḥanbalī text as setting no end to it (al-Mughnī 2:215). Ibn Taymiyyah held that a traveller shortens as long as he is travelling, even if he stays somewhere for months (Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā 24:18).

    Combining (jamʿ)

    Combining means praying Ẓuhr and ʿAṣr together, or Maghrib and ʿIshāʾ together, in the time of the first (early) or the second (late). Fajr is never combined. The Prophet ﷺ, when he set off before noon, delayed Ẓuhr until the time of ʿAṣr and prayed them together (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1111), and he combined Maghrib and ʿIshāʾ when he was in a hurry on a journey (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1106).

    • Ḥanafī: two prayers are not combined in the time of one, at home or on a journey, except at ʿArafah and Muzdalifah during Ḥajj (al-Mabsūṭ 1:149; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218). Ibn Masʿūd said he never saw the Prophet ﷺ pray a prayer outside its time except at Muzdalifah (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1682). Al-Sarakhsī explains the travel reports as combining “in action, not in time”: the first prayer at the end of its time and the second at the start of its own (al-Mabsūṭ 1:149).
    • Mālikī: Khalīl lists combining Ẓuhr and ʿAṣr as a concession for travel by land, even on a journey shorter than the shortening distance and without hurrying, while noting that al-Mudawwanah makes hurrying a condition. Whether the traveller combines early or late depends on when he expects to stop (Khalīl p. 44).
    • Shāfiʿī: combining is allowed on a journey long enough to shorten, early or late, and on a shorter journey in one view; if you are travelling during the first prayer’s time, delaying is better, otherwise the reverse (Minhāj p. 45). Early combining has three conditions: praying the first prayer first, intending to combine, and praying them one straight after the other. Late combining does not require these, but the first prayer must be delayed with the intention of combining (Minhāj p. 46).
    • Ḥanbalī: combining two prayers in the time of one of them on a journey is allowed (al-Mughnī 2:200). Ibn Qudāmah reports two narrations on whether combining or praying each in its time is better (al-Mughnī 2:200).

    Ibn ʿAbbās also reported that the Prophet ﷺ once combined in Madinah, with no fear and no journey (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 705). Scholars explain this report in different ways, and the schools don’t treat it as a general permission.

    Sunnah prayers on a journey

    Ibn ʿUmar said that on his journeys with the Prophet ﷺ he did not see him pray the voluntary prayers attached to the obligatory ones (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1101; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 689). The schools differ on how far this goes, and on which voluntary prayers a traveller should still keep.

    Putting it together at the airport

    • Know which school you follow, or ask your local scholar before the trip.
    • If your school allows combining, a long layover or an early flight may let you pray two prayers together in an airport prayer room. If it doesn’t, plan to pray each prayer in its time, on the ground or on the plane.
    • “Allah intends for you ease,” said in the verse on fasting while travelling (Qurʾān 2:185). These are concessions to use with knowledge, not a burden.

    Your own situation

    This page reports what the sources and the schools say. It is not a fatwā. Where scholars differ, we say so, and we don’t choose for you. For your own case, ask your local scholar or imam.
