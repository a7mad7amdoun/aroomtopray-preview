Praying while travelling

Praying in public

Sometimes there is no prayer room, or it is too far from your gate. Praying in a quiet corner of the terminal is normal for many Muslim travellers. A little planning keeps it calm for you and for everyone around you.

    The earth is a place of prayer

    The Prophet ﷺ said the earth had been made for him a place of prayer, so that whoever is reached by the time of prayer may pray where they are (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 335). The religion is easy, and no hardship has been placed on us in it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 39; Qurʾān 22:78).

    Choosing a spot

    • Check for a prayer room or multi-faith room first. Our airport pages list them, with whether they are before or after security.
    • Pick somewhere out of the flow of people: away from walkways, gate queues, escalators, doors and emergency exits.
    • Choose a spot where people won’t need to walk in front of you. The Prophet ﷺ warned against passing in front of someone who is praying (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 510), so it is kind to others not to put them in that position. A wall, a pillar or your bag in front of you helps.
    • Keep your bags with you and in sight. Unattended bags worry staff and other travellers.
    • Travel prayers are short, especially if you are shortening. A few calm minutes is all it takes.

    If staff or security ask

    Staff are usually asking because they don’t know what is happening, or because of where you are standing. A calm, short answer works best. Some wording you can use before you start or after you finish:

    “I’m praying. It takes about five minutes. Is there a better place for me to do it?”

    “Sorry, I didn’t mean to block the way. I’ll move over there.”

    “Is there a prayer room or a quiet room in this terminal?”

    If someone speaks to you while you are praying, know what the sources say about talking in prayer. The Prophet ﷺ told a man who had spoken during the prayer that “talking to persons is not fitting during the prayer” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 537). In al-Hidāyah, speaking in the prayer, deliberately or by mistake, invalidates it (al-Hidāyah 1:62). In the Minhāj, it is invalidated by speech, with a small amount excused if it slips out, if you forgot you were praying, or if you did not know it was forbidden; three consecutive steps also count as excessive movement (Minhāj p. 32). Airport and airline safety instructions still need to be followed. Whether a prayer interrupted in this way has to be started again depends on your school; ask your local scholar.

    If you are asked to move, moving is usually the quickest way to pray in peace. If you feel you were treated unfairly, note the time, place and what was said, and use the channels below afterwards.

    On the plane

    On an aircraft, follow the crew’s instructions. In the United States, it is a federal crime for someone on an aircraft to interfere with a crew member’s or flight attendant’s duties by assaulting or intimidating them (49 U.S.C. § 46504). Ask politely, accept a no, and pray in your seat if you need to. See praying on a plane.

    At US security: what the official pages say

    This is a summary of official pages, read on 13 October 2026. It is not legal advice, and rules are different outside the United States.

    • Religious clothing and items. The TSA says it trains officers “to conduct screening with respect and care when handling religious items,” and that loose or bulky garments and head coverings may get extra screening, including a pat-down (TSA, “Religion”).
    • Pat-downs. The TSA says a pat-down is done by an officer of the same sex, and that you may ask for private screening at any time, with a companion of your choice (TSA, “Security Screening”).
    • Repeated extra screening. DHS TRIP is the Department of Homeland Security’s contact point for people who have had difficulties at screening, including denied or delayed boarding and repeated additional screening (DHS TRIP).
    • Airline treatment. The US Department of Transportation takes complaints about unlawful discrimination in air travel by airline staff or contractors, including on the basis of religion. It asks you to give the airline a chance to resolve a problem first (US DOT, “File a Consumer Complaint”).

    If you think your rights were seriously breached, speak to a lawyer or a civil-rights organisation you trust.

    This page reports what the sources and the schools say. It is not a fatwā. Where scholars differ, we say so, and we don’t choose for you. For your own case, ask your local scholar or imam.
